A Sufi retreat is not a wellness weekend with Arabic calligraphy on the wall.

It is a few days given to remembrance — to prayer, dhikr, Qur'an, teaching and quiet — held in a place where the group can be undisturbed. The point is not to be entertained or improved. The point is to turn towards Allah with other people, without the ordinary noise that makes it so hard at home.

That has practical consequences for where you hold one. This guide covers what a Sufi retreat actually involves, how small groups and tariqas usually structure a stay, and what to look for in a venue if you are bringing a shaykh, a shaykha, a teacher or simply a circle of friends who want to sit together in remembrance.

## What makes a retreat a Sufi retreat

The outward shape is simple. A group gathers in one place for a few days. There is prayer at its proper times. There is dhikr, alone and together. There is Qur'an, recited and listened to. There is sohbet — teaching, conversation, questions. There is food shared at a table. And around all of it there is silence, or at least the absence of everything that usually fills the silence.

What distinguishes it is intention. A retreat becomes a Sufi retreat because the group has come to remember Allah, to turn inward, to sit with a teacher, and to soften the heart — not because the venue has a certain aesthetic or because anyone has booked a spa treatment.

That means the venue's job is mostly to remove obstacles: to provide clean space for prayer, to feed people well, to keep the world at a distance, and then to get out of the way.

## The rhythm of a Sufi retreat

Every group holds its retreat differently, but a pattern tends to emerge over a few days and it usually looks like this.

**Fajr** is the hinge of the day. In a quiet house in the countryside, the group wakes before dawn without alarms competing with traffic. Some pray together, some alone. Afterwards, many people go back to sleep, and that is fine — a retreat should not run people into the ground.

**Morning** brings the first real teaching slot. Sohbet after breakfast, when everyone is fresh, is often the most valuable hour of the day. Qur'an recitation, study or a reading may follow.

**Midday** is for Dhuhr, lunch, rest and walking. Time outdoors matters more than organisers expect. In rural Norfolk most guests end up on footpaths and field edges without being asked to.

**Afternoon** is often open: more teaching, a study circle, 1-to-1 conversation with the teacher, or sleep. Circles that try to fill every hour usually find that attendance thins by day two. Space is part of the teaching.

**Maghrib** returns the group to prayer, a meal, and then the evening.

**After Isha** comes the part most people remember: collective dhikr, Qur'an recited aloud, sometimes sacred sound or devotional poetry. In a house with no neighbours and no traffic, this can go on late without apology or anxiety. That is a real and underrated advantage of a rural venue.

## Why the countryside matters more than it sounds

Silence is not a luxury on a retreat. It is part of the method.

In a town, a retreat competes with noise: doors, cars, delivery vans, other guests, the temptation of a high street. In a country house surrounded by fields, the ambient noise drops away within an hour of arrival, and people notice. Conversation slows. Sleep improves. Recitation carries across a room differently.

The land also gives the group somewhere to go when it needs to be alone. A garden, a field edge, a bench under a tree — these matter in a house where a dozen people are sharing common space for four days. Having somewhere to withdraw is what makes being together sustainable.

## Zawiyya and khalwa: space within a retreat

Some groups want time held in the traditions of the zawiyya — a lodge where the community gathers for dhikr and teaching. Others want the possibility of khalwa, a period of seclusion and retreat for an individual, under guidance.

A venue can support both, but only if it has the physical room for them. A house that is entirely open-plan makes seclusion difficult. A house with a small sitting room, a spare bedroom or a garden building makes it possible. If this matters to your group, ask about it directly and explain what you need; the answer will tell you a great deal about how flexible the venue really is.

## Bringing a shaykh, shaykha or teacher

Most Sufi retreats in the UK are arranged by a teacher and a handful of students, not by a venue. The teacher finds a house, the students fill it, and the stay is shaped around the teacher's guidance.

For that to work well, the venue should be genuinely happy to hand over the rhythm of the week. Meals at times that fit the programme. The main room available late. No competing activities, no imposed schedule, no staff hovering.

It also helps when the host understands the adab of hosting a spiritual gathering: that the teacher is not a paid entertainer, that quiet is part of the hospitality, and that a group in dhikr should not be walked through on a fire-safety tour at the crucial hour.

And there is an older point in the tradition of hospitality: he who hosts a person of knowledge hosts what they carry. A host who knows that will look after the teacher's practical needs cheerfully and then leave them in peace.

## Women's Sufi gatherings

Many retreats are held by and for women, and they have particular requirements: a fully private house, including outdoor space, so that women can sit outside without hijab; private prayer space; a woman able to lead or teach; and enough room for both gathering and time alone.

Small venues suit these gatherings far better than large centres, partly because exclusivity is achievable — the whole house, the whole garden, the whole week.

## What a stay can include

A well-held retreat gives a group room for:

- daily salah, prayed with attention rather than squeezed between activities
- collective dhikr, sometimes in the early hours
- Qur'an recitation and listening
- teaching and sohbet, in formal sessions and in unplanned conversations
- sacred sound, devotional singing and praise, where the group keeps those traditions
- quiet: silence, rest, walking, journaling
- shared meals, eaten at a table rather than in transit
- 1-to-1 spiritual conversation, or therapeutic and healing sessions by arrangement

That last item is worth noting. Sufi healing has traditionally been understood alongside the care of the whole person — prayer, rest, food, company and time. A retreat that leaves space for individual attention can be more restorative than one with a full timetable.

## Food, hospitality and the atmosphere of a house

Meals do a lot of quiet work on a retreat. They are where people come together without being asked to; where a teacher talks informally; where a newcomer stops feeling like a stranger.

For a Muslim gathering the essentials are simple. Halal food, cooked with care, shared at a common table, with no alcohol in the house. But the character of the food matters too. Nourishing, seasonal, simple food supports early rising, long evenings of dhikr and clear mornings in a way that heavy catering does not. Garden-to-table cooking is not a fashion statement in this context — it is the practical difference between a group that feels well and a group that feels sluggish.

## Practicalities to settle before you book

If you are organising a Sufi retreat, settle these early:

- **Exclusivity** — is the house yours alone for the duration?
- **Prayer space** — a dedicated room, with somewhere for wudu, and quiet at Fajr.
- **Late use** — can dhikr continue after midnight without disturbing anyone?
- **Food** — fully halal, no alcohol on site, able to handle allergies and specific dietary needs.
- **Capacity and rooms** — how many guests sleep comfortably, and in what configurations?
- **Teaching room** — suitable for a circle, with acoustics that work without amplification.
- **Outdoor space** — including private outdoor space if the group is women-only.
- **Cost** — what is included, what is charged separately, and what the deposit arrangement is.
- **Travel** — how guests will actually arrive, and what the last mile involves.

## How to prepare for a Sufi retreat

Preparation is more inward than practical. Bring fewer things than you think, and make some room in yourself for what the days will ask of you.

Practically: comfortable clothing for sitting on the floor, layers for cold mornings, a prayer mat if you have a favourite, a notebook, and any medication you need. Set an out-of-office message and tell people you will be slow to reply.

Inwardly: arrive with an intention. Decide why you are going. If you are coming with a question, hold it lightly rather than demanding an answer. Do not treat the retreat as a project to complete, and do not measure it by how much you felt. Some of the deepest effects of a retreat are noticed weeks later, at home, in ordinary moments.

It also helps to protect the days either side. Arriving at midnight with a suitcase and leaving straight into a work morning undoes much of what the retreat has built.

## Returning home

The return is part of the retreat. A few days of remembrance does not usually produce a dramatic transformation; it produces a looser grip on the noise, and a memory of what stillness felt like.

Take one or two small things back with you — a wird you keep, a habit of praying at Fajr without rushing, a decision about how much of the day belongs to your phone. Enough to keep the door open.

## Frequently asked questions

### What happens on a Sufi retreat?

Typically a few days of prayer, collective dhikr, Qur'an recitation, teaching and sohbet, shared meals and quiet time, held in a private setting where the group is undisturbed. The exact shape depends on the teacher or guide the group has come with.

### Can a small group bring its own shaykh or teacher?

Yes — and this is the most common way Sufi retreats are arranged in the UK. The group books a venue and the teacher holds the retreat. It is worth confirming in advance that the venue will fit meals, quiet hours and room access around the programme rather than its own schedule.

### Do you have to be part of a tariqa to attend a Sufi retreat?

Not necessarily. Some retreats are open, and many groups welcome sincere newcomers; others are held for an existing circle. If you are unsure, ask the teacher or organiser directly — that is a normal question and usually welcomed.

### Is dhikr allowed late at night at a retreat venue?

At a rural venue with no neighbours, usually yes, and this is one of the practical reasons groups choose the countryside. If it matters to your retreat, ask before booking rather than assuming.

### What makes a good venue for a Sufi retreat?

A quiet, private house with a dedicated prayer space and wudu facilities, fully halal food, no alcohol on the premises, a room suited to sitting in circle, permission to use the space late, and outdoor land where guests can walk or be alone. Size matters less than atmosphere and flexibility.

### Do these retreats run on fixed dates?

Some do, but many are arranged privately around a group's own dates. If you are bringing a teacher or gathering a circle, [enquire with your preferred dates](/contact) and ask what the house can accommodate.
